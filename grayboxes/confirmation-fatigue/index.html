Scenario

From a working control to request #38

The situationThe Nursing and Allied Health department’s Help Assistant (DHA) prepares advising appointment requests, including clinical-placement clearance meetings. The submit action sits behind user confirmation: a real control, not a prompt instruction. Every request shows a dialog (student, advisor, date, time, reason), and a staff operator clicks Approve or Decline.
Week 1The operator reads every dialog carefully, catches one error (the wrong advisor), and declines it. The system works as designed.
Week 4The operator has approved about 200 requests at about 2 seconds each, and hasn’t seen another error. They start clicking Approve without reading the details.
The incidentOn a Tuesday, request #38 has a student name that doesn’t match the student ID: a stale record. The operator approves it in 1.8 seconds. The appointment is booked for the wrong student, and the confirmation, with its clinical-clearance reason, goes to the wrong person.
The control didn’t fail. The human operating the control fatigued.

The department, students, and records are fictional.

The gap: the technical control works perfectly. The human layer is failing. No extra guardrail or IAM policy fixes this. It’s a governance problem.
Why this is a gray box problem

Partial visibility at every level

What the operator sees

A dialog with student, advisor, date, time, and reason: partial information.

What the operator doesn’t see

Whether the name matches the ID in the record · whether the record was recently changed · how many they’ve approved today without reading

What the builder sees

The trace (approved vs. declined) · the time between dialog and click (2 seconds probably means not reading)

What nobody sees without monitoring

The pattern: falling approval times, zero declines after week 1, no alert configured

Gray box insight: the operator has enough information to catch the error, but not enough situational awareness to know they should be looking harder.

Key takeaway

Only as strong as the human’s worst day

A control that depends on human attention is only as strong as the human’s worst day. Governance has to account for human factors, not just technical enforcement.
